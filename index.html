Every table on this page is built from two numbers. The level is how strong a player is. The map effect is how much a particular map suits them, better or worse than their own average. Add the two and you get the Est Elo each grid cell prints.

The raw material is public match history: every game these players have played since 2021, ranked and lobby, each record carrying the rating all ten players held at the moment they played it.

1 · The win rate is thrown away

Matchmaking hands you opponents of your own strength, so almost everybody sits near 50% however good they are. A win rate says the ladder is working; it does not say who is good.

What separates players is who they beat. So every game is priced by the opposition that was in it.

2 · How strong the opposition was

O = own rating + (enemy team average − own team average)

In a 1v1 that is just the opponent's rating. In a team game it credits a player for the help they had and charges them for the carry they did: play beside weaker allies and O goes up, hide behind stronger ones and it goes down.

3 · The rating that would explain the record

Elo has one formula. The chance a player rated R beats an opponent rated O is

E = 1 / (1 + 10(O − R) / 400)

400 points of gap is ten-to-one odds; 100 points is about 64%.

The performance rating is the rating that would make the record unremarkable — the R at which the expected wins come out equal to the real ones:

E₁ + E₂ + … + En = wins

Where every opponent happens to be the same strength this has a short form worth keeping in your head:

R = O + 400 × log₁₀(wins / losses) 7–3 against 1600s → 1600 + 400 × log₁₀(2.33) = 1747

4 · Measured against the player's own average

A performance rating is an absolute number, so it moves when a player improves. Games four years apart cannot simply be subtracted. Everything is therefore measured relative to the player's own rating in those same games:

edge = R − (their average rating while playing them)

The map's effect is then that edge on the map, minus the edge on every other map in the pool.

Two traps hide in that sentence. Both were live mistakes here, and both changed who the tables recommended:

  • Leave the map out of its own baseline. Compare a map against an average it is part of and the answer shrinks by exactly the map's share of the sample. A player with 96% of his games on one map would report an effect of zero by construction.
  • Subtract the rating of the day, not of today. Otherwise a map played during a bad year scores as a bad map. Before this was fixed, 85% of the gap between a player's form then and now was flowing straight into the map number.

5 · Believed only as far as the sample allows

Anything measured on twelve games is mostly luck. Each number arrives with a standard error, and is pulled toward zero in proportion to how much of it could be noise:

shown = raw × τ² / (τ² + se²)

τ is how far map skill really does vary from map to map, once the measurement error has been taken back out. It is re-measured for every roster and printed on the Teams tab — about 70 Elo.

A wide error bar drives the factor toward zero and the cell falls back to the player's level; a narrow one lets nearly the whole raw number through.

How far the survivor is from zero, counted in error bars, is z:

✔ ✘ |z| over 2 — treat as real
✱|z| over 1.8 — a hedge, not a finding
(none)inside the noise

6 · The level: two ladders, neither trusted alone

Each ladder gives an anchor. Half of it is the peak, because reaching a rating says something that losing it again does not erase:

anchor = (current rating + peak rating) / 2

The 1v1 and team ladders are different scales, so the 1v1 anchor is converted by a straight line fitted on the 24 499 players who hold both ratings:

team = 588 + 0.566 × 1v1
level = the higher of (team anchor + stack) and (converted 1v1 anchor)

Why the higher and not the average. An Elo rating settles where its owner wins half their games. Somebody 95–18 on a ladder has not finished climbing, so that rating understates them. Taking the higher means an understating reading can only lose the comparison — it can never drag a level down, so no minimum sample has to be invented.

Why look at both. A team rating is settled by the team: a carried player floats above their own contribution and a carrying one sits below it. The 1v1 ladder cannot be spoiled that way — but it is also not team-game skill. Neither is enough on its own.

Stack is a small correction for someone who always queues with the same partner. Two people who win and lose together drift toward one rating, and then the ladder can no longer say which of them is the better player.

7 · A third opinion that never reads your own rating

Both anchors are ladder ratings, so both inherit whatever the ladder gets wrong about one person. The arbiter is a different kind of reading altogether: one strength per player, fitted over 54 164 matches so that the stronger side tends to win — with that player's own rating never used to rate them, and everyone else in the game held at their published rating.

The level is then pulled toward it, by how much the arbiter has actually seen:

w = n / (n + 400) n = the arbiter's effective games level = level + (arbiter − level) × w

So 400 effective games move the level halfway, and under 50 the arbiter is not read at all.

Old games count less — but only for players who have changed. A game is weighed first by how far the player's rating has moved since it, and only then by its age:

w = 0.5^(|rating then − rating now| / 200) × 0.5^(days ago / 1460)

200 Elo of movement halves a game's weight, and so does four years of age.

Counting in days alone would charge the same discount to somebody who has gained 1000 points and to somebody who has not moved at all. Measured in Elo, a player who has stood still keeps their whole record.

8 · Est Elo, per map

Est Elo = level + map effect

What the mark after a number means:

(none)the player's own record on that map
°no usable record, so the map's group stood in — Open, Closed, Water and so on. The games count in the tooltip is still games on that map, which is how you see how thin the stand-in is
~nothing to go on, so the bare level, less an inexperience penalty (−90 under 5 games, −25 under 20, nothing after that)

That penalty is measured rather than assumed: players really do underperform their rating on a map they have barely played.

Everything here is on the team-ladder scale, which reads a few hundred points below the 1v1 rating of the same person. Do not compare an Est Elo to a 1v1 Elo.

9 · What is thrown away

  • anything under 8 minutes — those are drops, not games
  • anything that is not a classic random map: Empire Wars, Return of Rome, treaty, regicide, deathmatch, data mods, scenarios, altered resources
  • every map outside the tournament pool
  • lobby games whose name announces a format this tournament is not — 1v1, ffa, 3v3v3, shared control
  • games from before the mid-2022 team-ladder rescale, spotted by a recorded rating above the player's own all-time peak
  • the unranked rating a lobby game carries — each player's ranked rating is looked up instead

Lobby games also come in about 6.6 percentage points below what their ratings predict, because self-picked teams are far more lopsided than matchmaking. So ranked and lobby games are kept in separate pools, each with its own baseline, and combined afterwards. They can compare one map against another; they cannot speak to absolute level.

10 · What these numbers cannot do

  • One map cell is weak evidence. Measure a map on the first half of a career and again on the second, and the two agree at only 0.28. Under about 25 games read the direction only; under 10 nothing works at all, which is why those cells fall back to the level.
  • Nothing reaches a map never played. A group is often one big map wearing another map's name. The ° is there so that is never invisible.
  • The anchor leans high. Half of it is a peak, and a peak is the top of a noisy series. It is applied to everyone alike, so comparisons hold, but absolute levels are optimistic.
  • Flank and pocket cannot be measured. The start position is in no part of the match record, and the civ cannot stand in for it — most of these players roll random.
  • A rating measures what the ladder measured. Nobody here has a record of playing as this particular four, on a stage, against a team that prepared for them.

The full argument, with the measurement behind every rule, is in method.md; whether to believe any of it — the estimator scored against an arbiter that reads nobody's own ladder rating — is in validity.md.
